\section{Bayesian Knowledge Building}

\begin{frame}
    \frametitle{How Do You Decide If You Like a New Restaurant?}
    Imagine a new ramen restaurant just opened nearby.
    \pause

    \medskip
    \dq{How do you decide whether it's any good?}
    \pause
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Before you know anything: maybe you check the Yelp rating --- \textbf{2 reviews, 4.5 stars}
        \pause
        \item A friend eats there and says it was \textbf{just okay}
        \pause
        \item You eat there yourself and \textbf{really enjoy it}
        \pause
        \item You go back with friends, and it's \textbf{mediocre}
    \end{itemize}
    \pause

    \medskip
    Your opinion \emph{evolves} as you gather information. You didn't start from scratch each time --- you \hl{updated} what you already believed.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
    \frametitle{Bayesian Knowledge Building}
    This is exactly how Bayesian statistics works:
    \begin{center}
    {\small prior $\xrightarrow{\text{observe data}}$ posterior $\xrightarrow{\text{observe more data}}$ updated posterior $\longrightarrow \cdots$}
    \end{center}
    \pause

    \medskip
    \textbf{Yesterday's posterior becomes today's prior.} Each new piece of evidence updates your beliefs --- and with more data, your original prior matters less and less.
\end{frame}

\section{Bayes' Rule, Reinterpreted}

\begin{frame}
    \frametitle{Recall: Bayes' Rule for Events (Module 2)}
    Bayes' rule lets us reverse conditional probabilities:
    
    \[
    P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(B \mid A)\, P(A)}{P(B)}
    \]
    \pause

    Last time we reinterpreted this for a \emph{parameter} $\theta$ and \emph{data} $x$:
    \[
    \underbrace{f(\theta \mid x)}_{\text{posterior}} \;=\; \frac{\overbrace{f(x\mid\theta)}^{\text{likelihood}} \cdot \overbrace{f(\theta)}^{\text{prior}}}{\underbrace{f(x)}_{\text{evidence}}} 
    \]

\end{frame}

\section{Disease Screening, Fully Worked}

\begin{frame}
    \frametitle{Recall: Disease Screening (Lecture 31)}
    A rapid test for a disease with prevalence $P(D) = 0.01$, sensitivity $P(+\mid D)=0.99$, specificity $P(-\mid D^c)=0.95$. A patient tests positive.
    \pause

    \medskip
    Last time we jumped straight to the punchline: $P(H_0\mid +) = P(\text{no disease}\mid +) \approx 0.83$. \hl{Today, let's derive it.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
    \frametitle{Deriving the Posterior}
    \[
    P(D \mid +) = \frac{P(+\mid D)\,P(D)}{P(+\mid D)\,P(D) + P(+\mid D^c)\,P(D^c)}
    \]
    \pause
    \[
    = \frac{(0.99)(0.01)}{(0.99)(0.01) + (0.05)(0.99)} = \frac{0.0099}{0.0594} \approx 0.167
    \]
    \pause

    \medskip
    So $P(D\mid +) \approx 0.167$, and $P(H_0 \mid +) = 1 - 0.167 \approx 0.83$ --- exactly the number from Lecture 31.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
    \frametitle{Why Isn't the Posterior Higher?}
    The test is 99\% sensitive, so a positive result feels like strong evidence of disease --- yet $P(D\mid +)$ is still only about 17\%.
    \pause

    \medskip
    \begin{itemize}
        \item The \hl{prior} $P(D) = 0.01$ was small: most people are healthy.
        \item Even a very accurate test produces some false positives (5\% of the 99\% healthy population), and there are a lot more healthy people to draw false positives from than sick people to draw true positives from.
    \end{itemize}
    \pause

    \medskip
    \textbf{The prior matters!} A single piece of strong evidence isn't always enough to overturn a low prior.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
    \frametitle{Recall: Frequentist vs.\ Bayesian, Same Data}
    \begin{center}
    \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.3}
    \begin{tabular}{>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{4.7cm} >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{4.7cm}}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Frequentist} & \textbf{Bayesian} \\
    \midrule
    $p\text{-value} = P(+\mid H_0) = 0.05$ & $P(H_0\mid +) \approx 0.83$ \\
    A false positive this rare would be somewhat surprising if $H_0$ were true --- some evidence against $H_0$ & Even after testing positive, it's still more likely than not that $H_0$ is true --- essentially no evidence against $H_0$ \\
    \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
    \end{center}
    \pause

    \medskip
    Now that you've seen the full Bayesian derivation, you can see exactly why these two numbers --- both computed from the \emph{same} data --- answer genuinely different questions.
\end{frame}

\section{Sequential Updating}

\begin{frame}
    \frametitle{What If You Test Positive Again?}
    \dq{Suppose the patient takes the test a second time (independently) and it's positive again. What's $P(D \mid +_1, +_2)$?}
    \pause

    \medskip
    \textbf{Yesterday's posterior becomes today's prior:} use $P(D) = 0.167$ (the posterior from the first test) as the prior for the second update.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
    \frametitle{Updating Again}
    \[
    P(D \mid +_2) = \frac{(0.99)(0.167)}{(0.99)(0.167) + (0.05)(0.833)} \approx \frac{0.165}{0.207} \approx 0.80
    \]
    \pause

    \medskip
    After \textbf{two} independent positive tests: about an \hl{80\% chance} of disease.
    \pause

    \medskip
    A third independent positive test pushes it to $P(D \mid +_3) \approx 0.99$.
    \pause

    \medskip
    \textbf{Each new piece of data updates our beliefs.} With enough data, the posterior is driven by the evidence rather than the original prior --- exactly like the restaurant example.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
\frametitle{In the Bayesian Paradigm}

\begin{itemize}
\item We can acknowledge and use available information 
\pause
\item Combine our prior information with the data to generate knowledge / understanding 
\pause
\item Depending on how strong our priors are, and how sure we are about our data, we may come to different conclusions
\end{itemize}

\pause
\medskip

\textbf{Key principle:} with more data, the prior tends to matter less and the data dominates.
Bayesian and Frequentist analyses will often agree when there is a lot of data.

\end{frame}

\section{More Than Two Hypotheses}

\begin{frame}
    \frametitle{Example: Classifying a Gravitational Wave Signal}
    In 2017, LIGO detected a gravitational wave signal --- a ripple in spacetime from a violent astrophysical event.
    \pause

    \medskip
    \dq{What produced it?}
    \pause

    \medskip
    \textbf{Possible sources}, with prior probabilities based on expected rates:
    \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.2}
    \begin{center}
    \begin{tabular}{l l l}
    BBH & Binary black hole merger & $P(\text{BBH}) = 0.50$ \\
    BNS & Binary neutron star merger & $P(\text{BNS}) = 0.15$ \\
    NSBH & Neutron star--black hole & $P(\text{NSBH}) = 0.10$ \\
    Noise & Detector glitch & $P(\text{Noise}) = 0.25$
    \end{tabular}
    \end{center}
    \pause

    \medskip
    Bayes' rule works the same way with \hl{more than two} hypotheses --- we just compute a posterior for each one.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
    \frametitle{First Observation: Signal Duration}
    The detected signal lasts more than 1 second (``long'').
    \pause

    \medskip
    \begin{itemize}
        \item $P(\text{long} \mid \text{BBH}) = 0.20$ \quad (BBH signals are typically short)
        \item $P(\text{long} \mid \text{BNS}) = 0.90$ \quad (neutron stars spiral slowly)
        \item $P(\text{long} \mid \text{NSBH}) = 0.50$
        \item $P(\text{long} \mid \text{Noise}) = 0.35$
    \end{itemize}
    \pause

    \medskip
    \[
    P(H_i \mid \text{long}) = \frac{P(\text{long} \mid H_i)\, P(H_i)}{\sum_j P(\text{long} \mid H_j)\, P(H_j)}
    \]
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
    \frametitle{Posterior After Signal Duration}
    \begin{center}
    \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.3}
    \begin{tabular}{l c c c c}
    \toprule
    Source & Prior & Likelihood & Product & Posterior \\
    \midrule
    BBH  & 0.50 & 0.20 & 0.100 & 0.268 \\
    BNS  & 0.15 & 0.90 & 0.135 & \textbf{0.362} \\
    NSBH & 0.10 & 0.50 & 0.050 & 0.134 \\
    Noise & 0.25 & 0.35 & 0.088 & 0.235 \\
    \midrule
    & & Total: & 0.373 & 1.000 \\
    \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
    \end{center}
    \pause

    \medskip
    BNS rose from 15\% to 36\%, while BBH dropped from 50\% to 27\% --- but there's still substantial uncertainty.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
    \frametitle{Second Observation: Electromagnetic Counterpart}
    Astronomers detect a \hl{kilonova} at the source location --- a burst of light produced when neutron star matter is ejected during a merger.
    \pause

    \medskip
    \begin{itemize}
        \item $P(\text{EM} \mid \text{BBH}) = 0.01$ \quad (black holes produce no light)
        \item $P(\text{EM} \mid \text{BNS}) = 0.80$ \quad (neutron mergers produce kilonovae)
        \item $P(\text{EM} \mid \text{NSBH}) = 0.20$
        \item $P(\text{EM} \mid \text{Noise}) = 0.02$ \quad (coincidental)
    \end{itemize}
    \pause

    \medskip
    We use the posterior from before as our new prior and update again --- \hl{sequential updating}.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
    \frametitle{Posterior After EM Counterpart}
    \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.3}
    \begin{tabular}{l c c c c}
    \toprule
    Source & Prior (from before) & Likelihood & Product & Posterior \\
    \midrule
    BBH  & 0.268 & 0.01 & 0.003 & 0.008 \\
    BNS  & 0.362 & 0.80 & 0.290 & \textbf{0.894} \\
    NSBH & 0.134 & 0.20 & 0.027 & 0.083 \\
    Noise & 0.235 & 0.02 & 0.005 & 0.014 \\
    \midrule
    & & Total: & 0.324 & 1.000 \\
    \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
    \pause

    \medskip
    After incorporating the EM counterpart, we're now 89\% confident this is a binary neutron star merger --- exactly how GW170817 was identified.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
    \frametitle{Bayesian Knowledge Building in Action}
    \begin{center}
    \begin{tabular}{l c c c}
    \toprule
    Source & Prior & After duration & After EM \\
    \midrule
    BBH  & 0.50 & 0.27 & 0.01 \\
    BNS  & 0.15 & 0.36 & \textbf{0.89} \\
    NSBH & 0.10 & 0.13 & 0.08 \\
    Noise & 0.25 & 0.24 & 0.01 \\
    \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
    \end{center}
    \pause

    \medskip
    Same pattern as the restaurant and disease examples --- each observation updates the \emph{full} distribution over all the hypotheses at once.
\end{frame}

\section{Summary}

\begin{frame}
\frametitle{Summary}

\begin{itemize}
\item The \hl{Bayesian approach} combines prior beliefs with data to produce updated beliefs (the posterior) --- just like how you naturally update your opinions.
\pause
\item \hl{Bayes' rule} lets us reverse conditional probabilities:
    \[
    \underbrace{f(\theta \mid x)}_{\text{posterior}} \;=\; \frac{\overbrace{f(x\mid\theta)}^{\text{likelihood}} \cdot \overbrace{f(\theta)}^{\text{prior}}}{\underbrace{f(x)}_{\text{evidence}}} 
    \]
\pause
\item We can \hl{update our beliefs incrementally} sas new evidence arrives 
\pause 
\item As we gather more data, the \hl{posterior is increasingly driven by the data} rather than the prior.

\end{itemize}

\end{frame}
