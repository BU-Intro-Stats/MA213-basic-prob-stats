\section{Same Data, Two Lenses}

\begin{frame}
    \frametitle{Case Study: The Drug Trial, Revisited}
    Recall the Phase 1 drug trial from Lecture 34: $n=6$ patients, $x=2$ respond. We want to know about $p$, the true response rate.
    \pause

    \medskip
    We've now built up \emph{two complete toolkits} for answering this. Let's run both on the exact same data and compare what comes out.
\end{frame}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\section{The Frequentist Side}

\begin{frame}
    \frametitle{Point Estimate and Interval}
    \textbf{Point estimate (Lecture 32):} $\hat{p} = x/n = 2/6 \approx 0.333$ --- the MLE.
    \pause

    \medskip
    \textbf{95\% confidence interval} (the Module 3 recipe, $\hat{p}\pm z^\star SE_{\hat{p}}$):
    \[
    0.333 \pm 1.96\sqrt{\frac{(0.333)(0.667)}{6}} = 0.333 \pm 0.377 = (-0.04,\ 0.71)
    \]
    \pause

    \medskip
    \Note{The lower bound is negative --- impossible for a proportion! With $n=6$, we don't have nearly enough successes/failures for the recipe's Normal-approximation conditions to hold.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
    \frametitle{Hypothesis Test}
    The company will advance the drug only if $p > 0.15$: $H_0: p\le 0.15$ vs.\ $H_A: p>0.15$.
    \pause

    \medskip
    \[
    z = \frac{\hat{p}-0.15}{\sqrt{(0.15)(0.85)/6}} = \frac{0.333-0.15}{0.146} \approx 1.26, \qquad p\text{-value} \approx 0.104
    \]
    \pause

    \medskip
    Not significant at $\alpha=0.05$ --- but check the conditions first: $n p_0 = 6(0.15) = 0.9$, far below the ``at least 10 expected successes'' rule from Module 3. \textbf{This recipe's conditions aren't met.} The frequentist toolkit alone doesn't give us a trustworthy answer here.
\end{frame}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\section{The Bayesian Side}

\begin{frame}
    \frametitle{Posterior and Interval}
    \textbf{Posterior (Lecture 34,} Dr.\ Banerjee's $Beta(1,5)$ prior$\textbf{):} \; p\mid x \sim Beta(3,9)$, posterior mean $0.25$.
    \pause

    \medskip
    \textbf{95\% credible interval:} $(0.06,\ 0.52)$ --- computed directly from the $Beta(3,9)$ distribution, valid regardless of sample size.
    \pause

    \medskip
    \Note{No Normal approximation, no sample-size condition to check --- the Beta-Binomial posterior is exact for any $n$.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
    \frametitle{Bayesian Hypothesis Test}
    Same question, same data: $H_0: p\le 0.15$ vs.\ $H_A: p>0.15$.
    \[
    p_{\text{Bayes}} = P(p\le 0.15 \mid x) \approx 0.221
    \]
    \pause

    \medskip
    A 22\% posterior chance that $H_0$ is true is modest evidence against it --- roughly consistent with the frequentist $p$-value of 0.104, but computed as a direct probability statement about $p$, with no small-sample caveat needed.
\end{frame}

\section{Side by Side}

\begin{frame}
    \frametitle{Side-by-Side Comparison}
    \begin{center}
    \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.3}
    \begin{tabular}{l c c}
    \toprule
     & \textbf{Frequentist} & \textbf{Bayesian} \\
    \midrule
    Point estimate & $\hat{p}=0.333$ (MLE) & Posterior mean $= 0.25$ \\
    Interval & $(-0.04, 0.71)$ \emph{(invalid!)} & $(0.06, 0.52)$ \\
    $H_0: p\le 0.15$ & $p=0.104$ & $p_{\text{Bayes}}=0.221$ \\
    Conditions needed? & Yes, and they fail here & No \\
    \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
    \end{center}
    \pause

    \medskip
    Both point estimates are in the same ballpark, and both hypothesis tests reach the same qualitative conclusion (not enough evidence). But only the Bayesian toolkit gives a valid interval with this little data --- because it doesn't rely on a large-sample approximation.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
    \frametitle{Why Did They Differ Here?}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item With $n=6$, the frequentist CI/HT recipes rely on approximations that need reasonably large samples --- and this sample is too small.
        \pause
        \item The Bayesian posterior is \hl{exact} for any $n$, but it depends on a prior (Dr.\ Banerjee's $Beta(1,5)$) --- a different oncologist's prior would have given a different posterior (recall the five oncologists, Lecture 34).
        \pause
        \item Neither toolkit is ``right'' in general. \textbf{Knowing the assumptions behind each lets you pick the right tool --- or know when neither recipe applies cleanly.}
    \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\section{What If We Had More Data?}

\begin{frame}
    \frametitle{Recall: They Agree With Enough Data (Lecture 31)}
    Back in Lecture 31 we claimed: \textbf{with enough data, Frequentist and Bayesian answers tend to agree.} We've now built the tools to check that claim directly.
    \pause
    \medskip
    \dq{What would happen to our two intervals if this had been a bigger trial --- same 1-in-3 response rate, but with $10\times$ or $100\times$ as many patients?}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
    \frametitle{Same Rate, More Patients}
    \begin{center}
    \small
    \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.3}
    \begin{tabular}{l c c c}
    \toprule
     & $n=6$ & $n=60$ & $n=600$ \\
    \midrule
    $\hat{p}$ (MLE) & $0.333$ & $0.333$ & $0.333$ \\
    95\% CI & \textcolor{rubineRed}{$(-0.04, 0.71)$} & $(0.21, 0.45)$ & $(0.30, 0.37)$ \\
    Credible int. & $(0.06, 0.52)$ & $(0.21, 0.43)$ & $(0.30, 0.37)$ \\
    \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
    \end{center}
    \pause
    \medskip
    Same response rate throughout ($x/n=1/3$), just more patients. The frequentist CI becomes valid once $n$ is large enough, and the two intervals \hl{converge} --- by $n=600$ they're nearly identical, regardless of which prior we started with.
\end{frame}

\section{Case Study: Overdispersed Counts}

\begin{frame}
    \frametitle{Real Models Get More Complicated}
    Everything so far in Module 5 had a closed-form answer: $\hat{p}=x/n$, $Beta(\alpha,\beta)\to Beta(\alpha+x,\beta+n-x)$. Real applied models rarely do --- let's see what that actually looks like.
    \pause

    \medskip
    \dq{A small business tracks \textbf{likes per post} on 200 recent posts. How should we model the number of likes?}
    \pause

    \medskip
    A natural first guess: $Binomial$ counts of ``successes'' suggest \hl{Poisson}, our go-to model for counts (Module 2).
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
    \frametitle{Does Poisson Fit?}
    For a Poisson model, the mean and variance must be \emph{equal}. Checking the data:
    \pause
    \[
    \bar{x} = 21.9 \qquad s^2 = 151.8 \qquad \left(\text{ratio} \approx 7\right)
    \]
    \pause

    \medskip
    The variance is about $7\times$ the mean. \textbf{This data is far more spread out than Poisson allows} --- some posts flop, some go semi-viral, and no single rate $\lambda$ captures both.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
    \frametitle{The Poisson MLE, Fit and Checked}
    The Poisson MLE is still just $\hat\lambda = \bar{x}$ --- closed form, from Lecture 32.
    \begin{center}
        \includegraphics[width=0.62\textwidth]{poisson_fit.pdf}
    \end{center}
    The fitted curve is far too narrow: it badly underestimates how often posts get very few \emph{or} very many likes.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
    \frametitle{The Negative Binomial Distribution}
    The \hl{Negative Binomial} distribution adds a second parameter $r$ controlling dispersion, on top of the mean $\mu$:
    \[
    P(X=x) = \frac{\Gamma(x+r)}{\Gamma(r)\,x!}\left(\frac{r}{r+\mu}\right)^r\left(\frac{\mu}{r+\mu}\right)^x, \qquad x=0,1,2,\ldots
    \]
    \pause
    \medskip
    \[
    E(X) = \mu \qquad Var(X) = \mu + \frac{\mu^2}{r}
    \]
    \pause
    \medskip
    Small $r$ = highly overdispersed; as $r\to\infty$, this reduces back to Poisson ($Var\to\mu$).
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
    \frametitle{A Model With a Dispersion Parameter}
    \begin{center}
        \includegraphics[width=0.88\textwidth]{nb_shapes.pdf}
    \end{center}
    \pause
    \medskip
    Same mean throughout ($\mu=20$) --- only the shape changes: a long right tail when $r$ is small, converging to the familiar Poisson bell shape as $r$ grows.
    \pause

    \medskip
    \textbf{The catch:} maximizing $L(\mu, r)$ has no closed-form solution for $\hat{r}$ --- we need \hl{numerical optimization} (e.g.\ \texttt{optim()} in R), exactly the idea from Lecture 32, just without a formula to fall back on.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
    \frametitle{The Negative Binomial MLE, Fit and Checked}
    Numerically maximizing $L(\mu,r)$ gives $\hat\mu \approx 21.9$, $\hat{r}\approx 3.3$.
    \begin{center}
        \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{poisson_vs_nb.pdf}
    \end{center}
    The Negative Binomial captures the spread that Poisson missed.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
    \frametitle{Frequentist Uncertainty: No Formula, So Simulate}
    There's no simple formula for $SE(\hat{r})$ here either. So we \hl{simulate}: repeatedly generate fake data from the fitted model, refit, and see how much the estimate bounces around.
    \pause

    \medskip
    \begin{center}
        \includegraphics[width=0.48\textwidth]{boot_mu.pdf}
    \end{center}
    This gives a 95\% interval directly from the simulated spread --- the same logic as the confidence intervals in Modules 3--4, just computed by simulation instead of a formula.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
    \frametitle{The Bayesian Side: No Conjugate Prior Either}
    Put priors on $\mu$ and $r$, likelihood $=$ Negative Binomial. \hl{No conjugate family here} --- unlike the Beta-Binomial, there's no clean formula for the posterior.
    \pause

    \medskip
    Instead: \hl{Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC)}. In its simplest form (Metropolis--Hastings), we take a random walk through parameter space, always moving to more-likely values, sometimes accepting a move to a less-likely one --- and the resulting chain of visited values \emph{is} a sample from the posterior.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
    \frametitle{Watching the Chain Explore}
    \begin{center}
        \includegraphics[width=0.42\textwidth]{mcmc_trace.pdf}
    \end{center}
    Each trace bounces around a stable range rather than drifting --- a sign the chain is exploring the posterior, not still searching for it.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
    \frametitle{The Posterior Distributions}
    \begin{center}
        \includegraphics[width=0.62\textwidth]{posterior_size.pdf}
    \end{center}
    Every value on the $r$-axis with posterior mass is a value we believe is plausible given the data --- no simulate-and-refit needed, the uncertainty is built into the posterior directly.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
    \frametitle{Same Data, Two Machines}
    \begin{center}
        \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{freq_vs_bayes_final.pdf}
    \end{center}
   
    The simulation-based confidence interval and the MCMC-based credible interval land almost exactly on top of each other --- \hl{different machinery, same answer}, because with $n=200$ posts, there's plenty of data.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
    \frametitle{What Carried Over}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Frequentist:} when $L(\theta)$ has no clean maximum, find $\hat\theta$ with \hl{numerical optimization}; study its sampling distribution by \hl{simulation} instead of a formula.
        \pause
        \item \textbf{Bayesian:} when the posterior has no clean closed form, approximate it by \hl{sampling} from it (e.g.\ MCMC) instead of deriving it.
    \end{itemize}
    \pause

    \medskip
    The core ideas --- likelihood, priors and posteriors, sampling distributions --- are exactly what you learned this module. Only the computation gets harder.
\end{frame}

\section{Summary}

\begin{frame}
    \frametitle{Module 5 Summary}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{LO1, Frequentist:} construct estimators via maximum likelihood; evaluate them via their sampling distribution (bias, standard error, MVUE).
        \pause
        \item \textbf{LO2, Bayesian:} update a prior into a posterior via Bayes' rule; update sequentially, order doesn't matter.
        \pause
        \item \textbf{LO3, Comparing:} Frequentist and Bayesian answer different questions about the same data, and can disagree --- especially with limited data or when a recipe's conditions aren't met.
        \pause
        \item \textbf{LO4--LO5:} discrete Bayesian models and the Beta-Binomial model both instantiate the same posterior $\propto$ likelihood $\times$ prior pattern.
    \end{itemize}
\end{frame}
